I applied to be a professor at Diablo Valley College around the same time I started building Shovels. Part of what attracted me to teaching was the responsibility of being at the front of the room. There's a performance to it. The students are depending on what I bring that day.

It feels like running a business in some ways, except there's no team. It's just me.

I wanted practice speaking in public and explaining complex concepts to a wide range of people. I also wanted to contribute to a broader community and get to know it. Teaching offered all of that.

As Shovels grew, the challenges became people problems as well as technical problems. My own performance and the team's performance are connected. If I want the team to get better, I need to get better too.

About a year ago, I was pretty anti-coaching. Investors introduced me to coaches, and I interviewed some. I thought they were too expensive and I didn't think they would help. Maybe I was right at the time.

But as Shovels grew, my thinking changed. I wanted to be the best leader I could be for this company. An entrepreneur I admire referred me to Leaders in Tech, or LIT. I applied and was accepted.

I know a little about how I learn. With guitar, I learn best by jamming with different musicians. Books didn't get me very far with Ruby on Rails. Having a project I was desperate to build did.

The T-group felt like that same kind of learning applied to leadership. Learning by doing, with other people. That was much more appealing to me than spending an hour with a coach and filling out personality surveys. That's a preference about how I learn, not a claim that coaching or those surveys can't help someone else.
