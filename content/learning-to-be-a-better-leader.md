I applied to be a professor at Diablo Valley College around the same time I started building Shovels. Part of what attracted me to teaching was the responsibility of being at the front of the room. There's a performance to it. The students are depending on what I bring that day, how we'll I'm prepared, and how confident I am on my feet.

It feels like running a business in some ways, except there's no team. It's just me.

I started teaching because I wanted practice speaking in public and explaining complex concepts to a wide range of people. I also wanted to contribute to a broader community and get to know it. Teaching offered all of this.

In the early days, when I didn't know what would become of Shovels, teaching was my insurance plan. Now, it's more of a sanity check. It serves as a reminder of what entrepreneurship is and what it means to people just outside of my tech AI bubble. For my students, I hope my presence in the classroom is equally revealing: a glimpse of the other side, a VC-backed tech founder teaching entrepreneurship to students who aspire to open nail salons and cannabis farms.  

Importantly, teaching has offered me a glimpse into myself. I need to communicate better, be more real, use a wider pallette of emotion when I speak. Strive for honesty and transparency and try to turn that surface area into insights. 

My teaching commitment plateaued, but Shovels kept growing. As Shovels grew, the challenges became people problems as well as technical problems. I discovered that my own performance and the team's performance are connected. If I want the team to get better, I need to get better too.

But how?

About a year ago, I was pretty anti-coaching. Investors introduced me to coaches, and I interviewed some. I thought they were too expensive and I didn't think they would help. Maybe I was right at the time.

But as the complexity of our problems grew, my thinking changed. I no longer had the answers all the time. I needed to build a culture that was determined to make good decisions. I needed to be the best leader I could be for this company. An entrepreneur I admire referred me to Leaders in Tech, or LIT. I applied, interviewed, and was accepted.

I remember being skeptical when I first read about this program. It wasn't cheap (~$20,000) but it was cheaper than one-on-one coaching. Its name was hokey, but the roster of tech leaders that went through it was wildly impressive. It also helped that the program traced its origins to a popular course at the Stanford Graduate School of Business called Interpersonal Dynamics, or "Touchy Feely" as it was known to students.

The thing that convinced me I would love this was T-groups. 

I'm old enough to know how I like to learn. With guitar, I learn best by jamming with different musicians. I've taken lessons, I've bought lesson books, and watched hours of guitar YouTube. Nothing compares to playing in a band. A band is like a project. Let's learn the songs, memorize whether the verse or the bridge comes after the second chorus, hit those breaks on the intro vamp. There's no better way for me to learn. 

Programming was the same for me. Books didn't get me very far with Ruby on Rails. Having a project I was desperate to build did. That's the story of Toofr: I desperately wanted to learn to program, but it didn't make sense until I had to build a website that guessed business email addresses. The project helped me push through the learning curve. The project made it all click! 

T-groups are project-based learning for leadership. Learning by doing, with other people. That was much more appealing to me than spending an hour with a coach and filling out personality surveys. I'd learn by watching other leaders, studying their flaws and their feats. 

This was all just assumption. I'd need to attend the opening retreat to see if my assumption was correct.
